- **Authors**: Truman Hickok
- **Comment**: Project page: https://so101-bench.github.io/ ; code: https://github.com/5hadytru/so101_bench
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Low-cost arms such as the SO-101 have become the most common entry point to robot learning, yet no released policy performs spatially constrained, language-conditioned tasks on them out of the box, and existing SO-101 benchmarks run only on physical hardware with simple tasks and few held-out objects. We introduce SO-101 Bench, a simulated benchmark for language-conditioned spatial manipulation built on an Isaac Lab digital twin of a real SO-101 setup. It has four tasks scored as five families, 138 objects in seen, unseen-instance and unseen-class splits, 916 evaluation episodes with subsets for held-out appearances, rotate-to-fit planning and occlusion memory, and 3,629 simulated demonstrations on which every learned policy is fine-tuned under an equal compute budget. The twin ranks policies as the real robot does: five policies trained without real data, four VLAs and an LLM agent, keep their exact ranking when the same weights run on the physical arm in an unseen environment with unseen objects (Spearman rho = 1.00, MMRV = 0.000), although simulation overestimates success. Across nine VLAs and world-action models, the best learned policies score near 61%, with spatial placement, unseen object classes and rotate-to-fit planning remaining open. An LLM agent that writes code and hands grasping to a learned policy scores 83.2%, and an ablation shows that this lead comes from the choice of harness and the choice of language model.

- **Authors**: Maryam Akhavan Aghdam, Sudip Mittal, Noorbakhsh Amiri Golilarz, Shahram Rahimi
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Traditional multiple sclerosis (MS) image analysis has primarily focused on retrospectively detecting and segmenting lesions. This study investigates whether baseline FLAIR MRI can identify regions at risk for future lesion formation beyond population-level spatial patterns. We introduce FORESEE-MS, an evaluation protocol that uses a fold-specific population-prior atlas as a baseline and assesses predictions using pooled voxel-level discrimination (AUROC and AUPRC) and within-patient lesion localization (Recall@Top-K%), with patient-level bootstrap confidence intervals. We evaluated five deep learning models (3D U-Net, Attn+ResBlock+VAE, ViT, and two SwinUNETR variants) using 5-fold cross-validation on 39 subjects from the MSSEG-2 dataset. The atlas achieved the highest pooled voxel-level AUROC and AUPRC, with no deep learning model reliably outperforming it on either metric. However, the 3D U-Net and Attn+ResBlock+VAE achieved higher mean voxel-level within-patient Recall@Top-K% than the atlas at all evaluated thresholds, although most paired confidence intervals for the differences included zero. These findings suggest that baseline FLAIR may contain patient-specific predictive information, but in this small cohort, it remains uncertain whether this information improves lesion forecasting beyond a population-prior atlas.



### A Heart Means Recursion: Exploring Visual Meaning and Communication in VLMs
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36449v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.AI**, cs.CL, cs.CV
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36449v1)

- **Authors**: Alexa R. Tartaglini, Zhengxuan Wu, Thaddäus Wiedemer, Martin Wattenberg, Been Kim, Yuxuan Li
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Do vision-language models (VLMs) share our ability to communicate visually? We share preliminary findings, starting from a surprising observation: simple visual symbols like a red heart can strongly influence model behavior on seemingly unrelated tasks, such as steering models towards recursive solutions on simple coding problems. Notably, this steering effect is largely implicit, as models' direct interpretations and thinking traces about visual symbols diverge substantially from their observed behavior. In visual communication between models, this leads to a generation--interpretation gap: while models tend to send shape primitives that they (and humans) find intuitive, these visual cues often fail to steer other models. These findings reveal that VLMs might be capable of rich, creative, and visually-grounded communication, but their interpretation and production of visual meaning diverges from human conventions in surprising ways. Together this work highlights both the promise and challenges of visual communication between humans and VLMs.

### Full Collapse Is Suboptimal: Controlled Partial Neural Collapse for Long-Tailed Recognition
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36460v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.AI
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36460v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 01:09:29+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 01:09:29+00:00
- **Authors**: Hyeontaek Hwang, Cheonghwan Hur, Daeyoung Kim
- **Comment**: Accepted at WACV 2027
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Recent long-tailed recognition (LTR) methods incorporate neural collapse (NC) and align features to a fixed Equiangular Tight Frame (ETF) as a uniform target for all classes. However, forcing tail classes to fully collapse over-contracts their samples and weakens generalization. To address this, we propose a controlled partial neural collapse, realized through two coordinated controls on a backbone: the Class-Adaptive Sparse Activation (CASA) classifier and the ETF-constrained adaptive margin (ETF-AM) loss. Rather than forcing a uniform full collapse, this regime allows head classes to collapse along the ETF while tail classes retain within-class diversity. This prevents the tail from being forced into full collapse, which is suboptimal under imbalance. To set the dimensionality of collapse, CASA induces class-related node specialization through feature-conditioned gating so that tail classes occupy their own feature directions. For the clearance axis, ETF-AM applies a frequency-adaptive margin, with smaller margins for head classes and larger for tail classes. On CIFAR100-LT, ImageNet-LT, Places-LT, and iNaturalist-2018, our method achieves the best overall accuracy over prior methods. In the empirical analysis, within-class spread increases monotonically from head to tail classes, consistent with tail classes retaining variability rather than fully collapsing.

- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: We study 3D visual grounding in 3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS), where a referring expression should identify a target object as both a rendered 2D mask and a set of explicit 3D Gaussians. Prior methods typically extract 3D targets by applying heuristic thresholding or top-ratio filtering to dense primitive-level relevance scores. However, these scores often vary substantially across scenes and prompts, making such heuristics sensitive and leading to unstable targeting. ClusterSplat addresses this by reframing 3D target selection as cluster-level selection over scene-adaptive candidates. The method first learns instance-aware Gaussian features, forms feature-aware seed voxels, and merges neighboring seed voxels when a description-length criterion decreases, producing a scene-specific set of cluster candidates. On ScanRefer, ClusterSplat achieves the best rendered 2D segmentation and explicit 3D target metrics among the compared 3DGS baselines. It also obtains the best average Ref-LERF scores, supporting the same trend under fine-grained referring descriptions. On ScanNet v2 class name queries, ClusterSplat achieves the strongest rendered 2D results and the highest 3D matching accuracy, while maintaining competitive 3D IoU. ### Confident First, Reflective Second: Reinforcement Learning of Confidence-Preserving Reflection for Video Understanding
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.36467v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.MM
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.36467v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-27 01:23:41+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-27 01:23:41+00:00
- **Authors**: Zihao Lin, Jingyuan Liu, Yucheng Zhang, Yuguang Yao, Lifu Huang
- **Comment**: 18 pages, 8 figures
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Video models answer from sampled frames and may miss the decisive moment. A common remedy is to commit to an answer, re-examine the video, and confirm or revise it, a loop now mainly learned with reinforcement learning (RL). We find that RL erodes the initial answer, a phenomenon we term confidence erosion, where confidence means the label-verified reliability of the initial answer instead of self-reported certainty: because errors can be repaired later, a correct initial answer earns less reward, and the initial answer stalls even as the final answer's gain over it rises. We propose the principle confident first, reflective second: the initial answer must stay reliable on its own, above a floor set by a reference policy, and reflection may only add to it. Our method, CoFire, implements this principle with one mechanism for each erosion channel: reward incentives, parameter drift, and gradient interference. CoFire reaches 67.0% mean accuracy on Video-MME, MLVU, and LongVideoBench, 1.7 points above the strongest compared 7B method, and improves initial-answer and final accuracy over outcome-reward RL by 4.1 and 3.7 points on average: a stronger initial answer carries through to a stronger final one.

- **Summary**: Multimodal forgery detection is commonly studied under a static assumption: training attacks are expected to approximate those encountered after deployment. - **Summary**: Agentic life-science benchmarks give an agent evidence about a named entity and score an answer against a recorded outcome.
