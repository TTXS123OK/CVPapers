### Spatial Uncertainty Analytics for Budget-Limited Inspection of Hyperspectral Scenes
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00478v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.00478v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-31 00:11:31+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-31 00:11:31+00:00
- **Authors**: Ritesh Sharma, Yuichi Motai
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Land-cover maps classified from wide-area hyperspectral surveys are least reliable along class boundaries and in ambiguous regions, so their errors are spatially structured. We study how this structure can direct a limited verification budget: when a low-altitude UAV or a ground crew can visit only a handful of sites, where it should go depends on how classifier uncertainty is arranged across the scene and how a fixed sensor footprint tiles that arrangement. We estimate a per-pixel uncertainty surface from the disagreement of an ensemble of spectral classifiers and select sites by greedy, field-of-view-aware coverage of the uncertainty mass. On seven benchmark scenes, the highest-uncertainty decile of pixels is 2.6 to 7.9 times richer in errors than the scene as a whole, and the coverage greedy covers up to 57% of the classifier's errors with 15 sites, 1.7 to 18 times as many as uncertainty-agnostic sampling of the same labelled region. A non-overlapping selection of the footprints richest in uncertainty does equally well, so the gain comes from scoring whole footprints by uncertainty mass. The advantage persists under spatially blocked evaluation and with a spectral-spatial classifier, although disagreement then ranks errors less well. It requires restricting the surface to labelled land cover: over the whole scene, disagreement peaks on unlabelled ground, where inspections cannot be scored. The best policy for covering connected high-uncertainty regions depends on the scene and on the threshold that defines them, and Gaussian-process selection covers less uncertainty than the coverage greedy on every scene. The pipeline runs on commodity CPUs.



### A Straight Line Is Not a Camera Pose: Auditing Short-Clip Pose Benchmarks
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00482v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.RO
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.00482v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-31 00:14:11+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-31 00:14:11+00:00
- **Authors**: Xinyi Shi
- **Comment**: 34 pages (8-page main text and appendix), 7 figures, 21 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: The absolute trajectory error (ATE) is the standard measure of camera-motion accuracy, from SLAM to feed-forward 3D reconstruction. On the common Sintel protocol, a constant-velocity straight line using no images has a lower mean ATE than 10 of 11 recent models re-run with official code. Two properties of ATE's alignment explain this. First, it maps any constant-velocity prediction onto the best-fitting line through the true path, so the line scores the path's regression residual. A residual signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) criterion predicts when moving a prediction towards its constant-velocity fit lowers ATE. It gives the outcome for 95% of 1056 model-clip pairs, 98% of 792 held-out pairs and 97% of 1034 ScanNet pairs, and it separates Sintel (median SNR 0.86) from TUM, ScanNet and full-length Bonn and Wild-SLAM (2.7 to 27), where straightening raises every model's mean ATE. Second, position-only alignment ignores where cameras look: rotating a trajectory by 30 degrees against its orientations leaves ATE unchanged, yet worsens the reprojection of static points for every model on Sintel, TUM and ScanNet in the main setting, and the line, second by mean ATE on Sintel, places them worst by geometric mean: a lower ATE need not mean better geometry. Fitting the alignment's rotation to the predicted orientations gives pose-anchored ATE, a one-function change in ATE's units costing 0.17 ms per clip (ATE: 0.11 ms). It ranks every image-free reference below every model on Sintel, tracks the reprojection error under rotation and straightening, where ATE does not (Spearman 0.61 vs. 0.32), largely preserves the models' order, and ties 10-frame translational relative pose error (RPE) and the discernible trajectory error (DTE) on our rotation and reference tests. Code, predictions and results will be released.



### Selecting or Solving? What Best-of-N Verification Delivers in Video Reasoning
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2611.00507v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.00507v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-31 00:54:03+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-31 00:54:03+00:00
- **Authors**: Martin Q. Ma, Yuxiao Qu, Ruslan Salakhutdinov, Louis-Philippe Morency, Paden Tomasello, Juan Pino
- **Comment**: Accepted at NeurIPS 2026 VLM4WRD workshop
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Answering a complex question about a long video is a grounding problem: the answer turns on a few seconds of evidence, and vision-language models remain well below human accuracy at finding them. The standard way to spend extra test-time compute on this kind of video reasoning is best-of-N selection: sample several chain-of-thought answers from a fixed model, then let a second model re-watch the video and pick one. Such pipelines are reported by a single headline number, their margin over a majority vote. That number conflates two effects with opposite deployment consequences: the benefit the second model adds by selecting among candidates, and the benefit it would have supplied by solving the question itself. We separate them, measuring the Selection Increment (SI) -- selection accuracy minus the same verifier's accuracy with the candidates withheld -- alongside the margin over self-consistency, across five long-video benchmarks. The Selection Increment is largest for the weakest verifiers: a verifier that solves poorly on its own leaves the candidates the most room to help. Beating the majority vote follows the opposite profile: where the vote already sits near its ceiling no verifier we test improves on it; below that ceiling only the strongest do. On Video-Holmes, the two come apart on a single pool of candidates -- a weak verifier gains +19.3 points from the candidates yet lands 7.1 points below the vote, while the frontier verifier gains the least and beats the vote. The weakest verifier also shows a failure mode: on EgoSchema it picks by where an option sits in the list rather than by what the video shows. Read as a merit score, SI puts a verifier that fails to beat a majority vote at the top of its ranking; read as an audit, it says which of the two effects a headline number delivers. Code is available at https://github.com/facebookresearch/best-n-selection-video-reasoning.

- **Comment**: Accepted by IEEE Transactions on Circuits and Systems for Video Technology
- **Journal**: IEEE Transactions on Circuits and Systems for Video Technology (Early Access), 2026
- **Summary**: All-in-one image restoration aims to recover high-quality images from multiple degradation types using a single unified model. Recent methods improve unified restoration with hybrid attention, prompt learning, or routing mechanisms, but generally focus on enhancing degradation adaptivity while employing similar feature modeling strategies across network stages. Consequently, the distinct representation requirements of encoding and decoding under diverse degradations remain insufficiently explored. In this paper, we propose SASFormer, a Stage-Aware Selective Attention Transformer for all-in-one blind image restoration. SASFormer follows a stage-aware design principle: the encoder uses established hybrid channel-spatial attention to build robust content-preserving and degradation-aware representations, while the decoder employs adaptive selective attention for restoration-oriented reconstruction. To address the stage-specific requirements of decoder reconstruction, we develop Adaptive Selective Channel Self-Attention (ASCSA) and Adaptive Selective Spatial Self-Attention (ASSSA), enabling the decoder to dynamically identify restoration-relevant channel responses and spatial contexts. Specifically, ASCSA performs adaptive query-channel selection and attention masking, while ASSSA adaptively integrates spatial attention across multiple window sizes. Compact implicit prompts further modulate the selection process without requiring explicit degradation labels. Extensive experiments on 3-, 5-, and 11-degradation settings demonstrate that SASFormer achieves strong restoration performance with favorable parameter efficiency. Moreover, stage-wise representation analysis and ablation studies provide empirical support for the proposed stage-aware design and the effectiveness of decoder-side adaptive selection.

- **Summary**: Large gaps in seismic acquisition disrupt reflection events and hinder spectral recovery. - **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Structured channel pruning for convolutional neural networks (CNNs) plays an important role in pattern recognition and deep learning. Nevertheless, existing methods usually calculate channel importance scores based on the magnitudes of individual filters, making it difficult to distinguish shared redundancy from individual channel contributions. To tackle this limitation, we propose a novel structured channel pruning method termed bi-sparse matrix factorization (BSMF). Specifically, BSMF characterizes the convolutional weight matrix as output-channel coefficients over a shared basis with row-wise $\ell_{2,p}$-norm and column-wise $\ell_{2,q}$-norm regularization terms, respectively. After that, a compact CNN is obtained by reconstructing the convolutional filters from the learned factors and discarding the redundant channels. Extensive experiments on the CIFAR-10 and ImageNet-1K datasets demonstrate that our proposed BSMF achieves significant network compression while maintaining high reconstruction accuracy. ### Regularized Wasserstein Distance for Discrete Generative Modeling and Clustering Analysis

- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2611.00653v1)

- **Authors**: Senmao Ye, Fei Liu, Dawei Zhang, Yuqian Zheng, Jun Zhang, Zhonglong zheng
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: In this paper, we formulate clustering analysis as discrete generative modeling. Specifically, when the generator is restricted to a finite candidate space, the generated samples naturally emerge as the most representative prototypes of the underlying data distribution. To model such a discrete candidate space, we employ a Discrete Distribution Network (DDN). However, DDN suffers from unstable training due to its hard nearest-neighbor selection. To stabilize training and encourage geometric consistency, we replace DDN's l2 loss between images with regularized Wasserstein distance between the empirical distributions of real and generated images. The resulting framework, termed Discrete Generative Clustering (DGC), offers four notable advantages: 1) the learned cluster centers are directly interpretable, as they are realistic synthetic samples; 2) the inference cost scales logarithmically with the number of clusters, i.e., O(log K), making the method scalable to extremely large K; 3) the regularized Wasserstein distance provides a smooth and well-behaved optimization objective that facilitates stable convergence, whereas DDN's l2 loss tends to converge to poor local optima; and 4) DGC replaces DDN's nearest-neighbor selection with random-neighbor selection, which avoids the density shift problem during multi-step forward. Extensive experiments on several benchmarks demonstrate the effectiveness and desirable properties of DGC. The code is publicly available at GitHub.

- **Summary**: End-to-end planners decode the ego trajectory either by coordinate regression, which is fast but blends plausible maneuvers into one trajectory that may follow none of them, or by diffusion and flow matching, which model the trajectory distribution but need several inference steps. Drifting models move this iteration into training: a one-step generator learns to follow an attraction--repulsion field between generated and ground-truth samples. Drifting was developed for class-conditional image generation, where every class supplies many positive samples. In planning, every scene is its own condition. Pooling scenes would attract the generated trajectories of one scene to the ground truth of unrelated scenes, whereas computing the field per scene leaves a single positive, the expert trajectory. Mutual repulsion then holds the generated trajectories on a shell around it, so any single generated trajectory deviates from the ground truth. We present DriftingPlanner, a trajectory planner trained with the drifting loss alone, without any coordinate regression term. It computes the field per scene and removes the shell at inference: it decodes every noise vector together with its negation in one batched pass, and averaging each antithetic pair cancels the linear part of the displacement from the shell center. On NAVSIM navtest, DriftingPlanner reaches a state-of-the-art 93.75 PDMS, and with single-step inference its drifting decoder comes within 0.13 PDMS of the best diffusion or flow matching setting, which needs ten inference steps.
