### T-DIG: Text-Conditioned Diversity for Image Generation
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29003v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.29003v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-21 00:11:32+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-21 00:11:32+00:00
- **Authors**: Simran Khanuja, Rohit Gandikota, Gaurav Parmar, Graham Neubig
- **Comment**: under review
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Text-to-image (T2I) platforms typically return several images per prompt so users can explore different visual interpretations and choose one that matches their intent. However, T2I models are usually optimized for single-image quality which comes at a cost of multi-image diversity, limiting this exploration. Diversity is also contextual: one user wants variations in background, while another wants them in artistic style, and the same user may later want variations in clothing. The generated images should differ along whatever axis the user cares about. In this paper, we introduce T-DIG, a text-conditioned diversity framework for image generation and editing. T-DIG is an iterative framework comprising multiple modules that lets users type prompts, inspect default generations, request variations using natural language, and continue refining outputs until they're satisfied. At its core, T-DIG uses an LLM policy that produces K axis-conditioned prompts or edit instructions for a frozen generator or editor. We train this policy with GRPO using a text-conditioned diversity reward and alignment penalties. Because the image generator remains frozen, T-DIG applies to open-weight, distilled, and proprietary models. We also release the first dataset for text-conditioned prompt diversity in image generation, with the training and validation splits built from human-written axes and a test set drawn from real user interactions with T-DIG. T-DIG far exceeds baselines in improving text-conditioned diversity, while preserving base-prompt alignment across a variety of metrics and human judgements. We show that user-directed diversity exploration can be added to a wide range of image generation models, open and closed, without the need to access their weights. Website: https://image-study.org/t-dig/

- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.29025v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-21 00:59:03+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-21 00:59:03+00:00
- **Authors**: Yongsheng Lian
- **Comment**: 19 pages, 6 figures, 13 tables. Code, set lists and model weights: https://github.com/yongshenglian/foodmass-transfer
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Estimating food mass from a photograph is central to image-based dietary assessment, but whether trained models transfer to new cameras and foods is unclear. We fine-tuned DINOv2-base on 2,253 weighed Nutrition5k dishes to predict mass from one RGB photograph and compared it with a zero-shot vision-language model, Qwen3-VL-32B, on Nutrition5k and three external datasets: ASA24 studio portions, ECUSTFD whole foods and Food Portion Benchmark meal trays. In domain, the fine-tuned model's mean absolute error was 24.8 g, less than half of Qwen's. On external datasets it did not beat Qwen. For a food it had never seen, it ranked portions by size, but no better than a count of food pixels. Its gram scale was wrong in two ways: it changed with how much of the scene the photograph showed, and it was off by a different factor for each food. Training on other foods or trays from the tested dataset cut the external error by 38 to 65%. DINOv2-large, a larger backbone trained on the same dishes, did not reduce the external error. One labeled photograph of the same food cut the model's ASA24 error to 28.3 g, better than that photograph achieved alone. Prediction intervals that held the true mass for 90% of Nutrition5k dishes held it for at most half of external photographs. Nutrition5k often photographed the same plate several times, so a random test split shared near-identical photographs with training and overstated accuracy. Single-image mass estimators therefore need labeled photographs from the setting where they are used.

- **Summary**: Amodal video segmentation asks for the complete mask of an object in every frame, including the parts hidden behind other objects. The best current methods generate these masks with fine-tuned video diffusion models, which need many denoising steps and an extra depth network, and give a different answer for every sample. Self-supervised video world models of the JEPA family are trained on a closely related task: predicting the representation of masked space-time regions from the visible ones. Controlled experiments trace the gain to the predictor's pre-trained weights rather than to its capacity, for world models from 87M to 1.85B parameters. ### Diffusion Posterior Sampling with Uncertain Forward Models
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29102v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.29102v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-21 02:47:28+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-21 02:47:28+00:00
- **Authors**: Wentao Shangguan, Vivek K Goyal
- **Comment**: None
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Diffusion posterior samplers reconstruct signals by combining a pretrained diffusion prior with a measurement likelihood specified by a forward model and a noise model. Calibration and reference data can characterize uncertainty in the forward model's parameters, such as blur kernels or detector responses. Fixing these parameters at estimated values leaves their uncertainty out of the likelihood, and parameter errors are thus absorbed into the reconstruction. We introduce an uncertainty-aware likelihood (UAL), a Gaussian approximation to the marginal likelihood obtained by integrating over the uncertain parameters. For each candidate signal, UAL propagates parameter uncertainty into a structured measurement covariance, reducing the quadratic penalty along residual directions that parameter variation can explain. Existing samplers can use UAL in their data updates while retaining their pretrained diffusion priors and sampling schedules. Under a fixed Gaussian nuisance model, we bound likelihood error from linearization and average divergence between the exact marginal and UAL posteriors. We compare samplers with and without UAL across six inverse problems spanning Gaussian and motion deblurring, CT, MRI, ultrasound, and fluorescence microscopy, and find improvements in reconstruction and ensemble prediction. UAL reduces geometric mean MSE by 9% to 20% across samplers in the main Gaussian-deblurring setting and by 51% to 92% in motion deblurring when non-blind samplers use the population-mean kernel. In motion deblurring, DAPS and PnP-DM with UAL also give 6.2 to 6.4 dB higher ensemble-mean PSNR than published BlindDPS and Fast Diffusion EM. On the ablation set, UAL outperforms trace-matched isotropic inflation for PnP-DM in both deblurring tasks; in motion deblurring, it also exceeds the best-tested fixed scalar data weighting by 3.3 dB for PnP-DM and 4.2 dB for DAPS in average PSNR.

### DriftNeRF: Cross-Ray Density Drifting for Sparse-View Neural Radiance Fields
- **Arxiv ID**: http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.29106v1
- **DOI**: None
- **Categories**: **cs.CV**, cs.LG
- **Links**: [PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2610.29106v1)
- **Published**: 2026-10-21 02:49:34+00:00
- **Updated**: 2026-10-21 02:49:34+00:00
- **Authors**: Juntang Wang, Yihan Wang, Sheng'en Li, Hao Wu, Dongmian Zou, Shixin Xu
- **Comment**: 13 pages, 6 figures, 18 tables
- **Journal**: None
- **Summary**: Neural Radiance Fields (NeRF) synthesize photorealistic novel views from dense image collections, but degrade sharply when few views are available. We attribute a specific, measurable defect to the objective itself: the rendering loss is a sum over independent rays and contains no term comparing densities that different rays assign to the same region of space, so nearby 3D points can settle on contradictory densities while every training pixel is reproduced correctly. We make this quantity explicit by measuring cross-ray density disagreement directly during training, and show that it stays high in vanilla NeRF, falls only incidentally under frequency-based regularization, and moves together with held-out quality in the sparse regime. We then close the gap with DriftNeRF, a drop-in regularizer that instantiates the drifting-field template on density samples: a masked exponential kernel aggregates densities from nearby points on other rays into a stop-gradient consensus target, and the resulting drifting field penalizes unsupported disagreement while leaving agreed-upon boundaries intact. The regularizer reads only sampled positions, ray identifiers, and predicted densities, so it requires no pretrained model, external geometry, or additional images, and transfers across field parameterizations. On NeRF-Synthetic it reduces residual disagreement by an order of magnitude, and across NeRF-Synthetic, DTU, and LLFF it improves 3-view PSNR over the strongest MLP-NeRF baseline by 0.42-0.65 dB and remains competitive with sparse-view Gaussian pipelines under a matched protocol without their depth, flow, or covisibility preprocessing.

- **Summary**: Video world models predict future frames from past frames and actions. They serve as simulators in which agents train, plan or are evaluated, and that humans can control interactively. They generate in the latent space of a video autoencoder, whose compression sets their cost per frame and how long each action waits to take effect. Yet world models often reuse the autoencoders of text-to-video generators, built for offline generation with no agent waiting and, without their training data and code, not retrainable at another compression. We therefore ask how, where and how much a world model should compress. We build CADRE, an open family of causal diffusion representation autoencoders spanning multiple spatio-temporal compressions. Its members differ only in a small bottleneck on frozen features and share a diffusion decoder self-distilled to one or a few steps. We train the same world model on each autoencoder in two domains, manipulation and navigation, with two environments each. CADRE forms most of the latency-quality frontier, especially with a moving camera. Our study also leads to four findings. At strong compression, a diffusion decoder is sharper than perceptual regression. In robot manipulation, temporal compression is more effective in the autoencoder than in the world model. Manipulation and navigation call for opposite compressions: temporal compression costs little when the camera is still, and spatial compression when it moves. Surprisingly, with a still camera, coarser spatial tokens hurt motion far more than image quality. Code, models and a live demo are at https://kyutai.org/blog/2026-10-21-cadre
